# PIQ Form: Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Because the PIQ directly shapes your interview, mistakes here don't just look untidy on paper — they generate awkward questions you have to answer live in front of the IO. These are the errors candidates make most often.

## 1. Exaggerating Achievements

Listing a certificate of participation as an "award," inflating a casual weekend activity into a "leadership position," or rounding up marks/percentages.

- **Why it hurts**: the IO routinely asks follow-up questions on anything listed. An exaggerated claim that unravels under a simple "tell me more about that" damages credibility far more than a modest, accurate entry ever would.
- **Fix**: list things exactly as they are, and be ready to talk about them in detail rather than optimising the form for how impressive it looks on paper.

## 2. Vague or Generic Hobby Entries

Writing generic entries like "reading," "music," or "sports" with no specifics.

- **Why it hurts**: a vague hobby invites a simple probing question ("What book are you currently reading?") that a candidate who listed it just to fill space often can't answer well.
- **Fix**: be specific — "reading — currently reading a biography of [person]" or "football — right-back, played for my college team for two years" gives you something real to discuss.

## 3. Inconsistency With Other Documents

Marks, dates, or family details that don't match your certificates or other official documents.

- **Why it hurts**: this is one of the most common sources of the "clarify this inconsistency" question at the conference, and repeated mismatches can raise doubts about attention to detail or honesty.
- **Fix**: keep your certificates and ID documents on hand while filling the form, and cross-check every number.

## 4. Rushing Through Family Background

Treating the family section as a formality and filling it hastily with minimal detail.

- **Why it hurts**: this section is a common opener for interview conversation ("Tell me about your parents' occupations"), and a candidate who visibly hasn't thought about their own family background can come across as disengaged.
- **Fix**: fill it thoughtfully — you should be able to speak naturally about each family member listed.

## 5. Leaving Sections Blank Unnecessarily

Skipping the achievements or extra-curricular sections because "nothing comes to mind" under time pressure.

- **Why it hurts**: everyone has done *something* — a genuine hobby, a small responsibility, a school-level activity. An entirely blank section can look like a lack of engagement with life outside academics, which cuts against the profile the board is looking for.
- **Fix**: take a moment before the form is handed out to mentally list your genuine activities and interests, so you aren't drawing a blank under time pressure.

## 6. Copying a "Model" PIQ From the Internet

Using generic sample answers found online instead of your own genuine details.

- **Why it hurts**: the IO has read thousands of PIQs and can quickly tell a generic, templated entry from a genuine one — and if you can't speak convincingly to a copied answer, it becomes an immediate red flag.
- **Fix**: use guidance material (like this one) to understand *what* each section is asking for and *why* — but always fill in your own, real details.

## 7. Mismatched Tone Between the PIQ and Your Natural Personality

Writing overly formal, stiff, or "textbook" answers in open-ended sections that don't sound like how you'd actually speak.

- **Why it hurts**: the IO reads the PIQ and then spends 30–45 minutes talking to you directly — a jarring gap between the written tone and your natural speaking style can itself read as inauthentic, even if every individual fact is true.
- **Fix**: write in your own voice. A slightly less polished but genuine sentence beats a stiff, formal one that doesn't sound like you.

## 8. Forgetting to Prepare for What You Wrote

Filling the form carefully, then never revisiting it before the interview days later.

- **Why it hurts**: by the time your interview happens, some candidates have genuinely forgotten the specifics they wrote days earlier, leading to hesitant or inconsistent answers about their own form.
- **Fix**: as covered in the companion material on using your PIQ to prepare for the interview, treat your submitted PIQ as a document to revisit and reflect on, not something to forget the moment you hand it in.

## 9. Overloading a Single Section to Compensate for a Weak One

Writing an unusually long list of achievements to compensate for, say, a thin extracurricular record, hoping quantity offsets the imbalance.

- **Why it hurts**: a lopsided PIQ (impressive academics, nothing else) is a completely normal, honest profile for many candidates — trying to artificially balance it by padding one section usually just introduces more exaggerated or vague entries (see mistakes #1 and #2) rather than solving the actual concern.
- **Fix**: it's fine to have a PIQ that's stronger in some areas than others. Address any genuine gap through honest self-reflection in the interview, not by inflating the form.

## Quick Pre-Submission Checklist

- [ ] All personal and family details match my official documents exactly
- [ ] Every listed hobby/activity is something I can discuss in detail for at least a few minutes
- [ ] Every achievement is accurately described, with no exaggeration
- [ ] No section is blank without a genuine reason
- [ ] I've reviewed the whole form once more before submitting, reading it as an interviewer would
