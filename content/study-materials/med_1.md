# SSB Medical Examination: Complete Overview

Once the board recommends you at the conference, you move into the medical examination — a separate, thorough assessment conducted by military doctors, independent of the psychological and interview evaluation you've just completed.

## Where and When It Happens

- Conducted at a designated **Military Hospital** near the SSB centre, or occasionally at the SSB centre's own medical facility.
- Usually begins the same day as your recommendation or the following day, and can take **2–5 days** depending on the number of recommended candidates and the tests required.
- You will typically stay at the military hospital's accommodation for the duration.

## Structure of the Examination

The medical is conducted by a **Special Medical Board (SMB)** comprising doctors from multiple specialities. It generally covers:

- **General physical examination** — height, weight, build, and overall fitness assessed against Armed Forces standards
- **Eyes (ophthalmology)** — visual acuity, colour vision, and any refractive errors
- **ENT (ear, nose, throat)** — hearing thresholds, nasal septum, and related structures
- **Dental** — cavities, gum health, and alignment
- **Cardiovascular and respiratory** — blood pressure, chest X-ray, ECG
- **Pathology** — blood tests (haemoglobin, blood sugar, etc.) and urine tests
- **Orthopaedic** — joints, spine, and any old injuries or deformities
- **Skin** — visible scars, tattoos (subject to service-specific tattoo policies), and dermatological conditions
- **Psychiatric screening** — a brief assessment separate from the SSB psychologist's tests

## Outcomes of the Medical Board

- **Fit** — you are cleared and proceed toward final merit/selection based on your entry scheme's criteria.
- **Temporary Rejection (TR)** — a correctable or borderline issue (e.g., low haemoglobin, minor infection, marginally out-of-range weight) is flagged; you are given a window (commonly **42 days**) to rectify it and reappear before an **Appeal Medical Board (AMB)**.
- **Permanent Rejection (PR)** — a disqualifying condition under Armed Forces medical standards; you may still request a **Review Medical Board (RMB)**, though this is not granted automatically (see the companion material on SMB/AMB/RMB for details).

## A Typical Day-by-Day Flow

While the exact schedule varies by hospital and batch size, a common pattern looks like this:

- **Day 1**: Registration, general physical measurements (height, weight, build), and an initial round of pathology (blood and urine sample collection).
- **Day 2**: Eyes, ENT, and dental examinations — each conducted by the relevant specialist department.
- **Day 3**: Cardiovascular and respiratory checks (blood pressure, ECG, chest X-ray), plus orthopaedic examination.
- **Day 4–5**: Any follow-up examinations for borderline findings, consolidation of all department reports, and the final SMB decision.

Larger batches or centres handling multiple SSB boards simultaneously can extend this timeline; smaller batches sometimes complete in fewer days.

## How Findings Are Consolidated

Each specialist department examines you independently and submits its own findings; there is no single doctor who sees your "whole picture" during the examination itself. The **Special Medical Board**, typically headed by a senior medical officer, then consolidates every department's report into one of the three outcomes (fit, temporary rejection, or permanent rejection). This is why a single department's borderline finding doesn't automatically mean rejection — the SMB looks at the complete picture, and some minor findings across different departments are individually acceptable even if none of them alone would be.

## What to Bring

- Any existing medical records, prescriptions, or spectacles/contact lens details
- A list of any past surgeries, hospitalisations, or chronic conditions — declared honestly on the medical history form
- Comfortable clothing, since you'll be examined across multiple departments over several days

## Key Principle

The medical examination is **procedural and standards-based**, not subjective like the psychological assessment — every measurement is checked against a published table. The best preparation is addressing correctable issues (fitness, minor infections, dental cavities) well before you reach SSB, since there is no way to "perform" your way through a physical measurement.
