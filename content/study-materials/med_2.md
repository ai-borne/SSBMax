# Vision and ENT Standards for the Armed Forces

Vision is consistently one of the most common reasons for medical disqualification at SSB, and the exact standard depends heavily on which service and branch you're applying for.

## Vision Standards by Service

**Army (general entries):**
- Uncorrected vision: **6/6 in the better eye, 6/9 in the worse eye**
- Myopia (short-sightedness) limit: not more than **−2.5 D**, including astigmatism
- Corrective lenses are generally acceptable for non-flying entries within limits

**Air Force — flying branch (pilots):**
- Uncorrected vision: **6/6 in both eyes**
- Very tight tolerance: myopia not more than **−0.75 D**, hypermetropia not more than **+1.5 D**
- Spectacles are **not permitted** for pilot candidates; contact lenses and corrective surgery are also generally disqualifying for this stream
- This is the strictest vision standard across all entries, given the demands of flying

**Navy and non-flying Air Force branches:**
- Broadly similar to Army standards, with some branch-specific variation — always confirm the exact table for your specific entry and branch before applying

## Colour Vision

- Candidates are tested using **Ishihara plates**.
- Flying branches require the highest standard of colour perception (CP-I); ground/technical branches may accept lower grades depending on the specific role.
- Colour vision deficiency is **not correctable** and is assessed as-is — unlike refractive errors, there is no preparation that changes this outcome, so know your own colour vision status before choosing an entry stream.

## ENT (Ear, Nose, Throat) Standards

- **Hearing**: must meet minimum thresholds tested via audiometry; any hearing loss beyond the permitted range in either ear can lead to rejection.
- **Nasal septum**: a significantly deviated septum causing breathing obstruction may be flagged, though minor deviations are often acceptable.
- **Tonsils and adenoids**: chronic infection or significant enlargement can be a temporary rejection reason, often correctable with treatment before the appeal window closes.
- **Perforated eardrum** or chronic ear discharge is a common disqualifying finding — get any persistent ear issue examined well before your medical.

## Why the Standards Differ So Much by Branch

The variation between a general Army entry and Air Force flying branch isn't arbitrary — it maps directly to the physical demands of the role. A flying branch pilot operates in an environment where corrective lenses can fog, shift, or fail under G-forces and rapid pressure changes, and where even small perceptual errors have severe consequences at speed — hence the much stricter, uncorrected-vision-only standard. A general Army or ground-duty role has no equivalent constraint, so a wider, correctable range is acceptable. Understanding this logic helps you interpret standards you encounter for entries not explicitly covered here — the stricter a role's real-world visual demands, the stricter its medical standard will be.

## Testing Methodology You'll Actually Encounter

- **Visual acuity** is tested using a standard Snellen chart at a fixed distance, both unaided and, where applicable, with your correction.
- **Refraction** is measured objectively (often via autorefractor, cross-checked manually) to determine your exact dioptre power — this is why guessing or downplaying your own prescription doesn't help; the board's own measurement is authoritative regardless of what you believe your power to be.
- **Audiometry** for hearing is conducted in a soundproofed booth, testing your threshold across a range of frequencies in each ear separately — a habit of listening to loud music through earphones for years can, in some cases, show up here, which is worth being mindful of well before your medical.

## What You Can Do in Advance

- Get a full eye check-up (including a dilated exam) months before SSB, not the week before — this gives you time to address any correctable issue.
- If you already wear spectacles, know your exact power (in dioptres) and disclose it accurately; the medical board will independently verify it regardless.
- Treat any ENT infection, chronic sinusitis, or persistent ear discharge well in advance — these are frequently **temporary** rejections that are easy to avoid with timely medical attention.
- Avoid vision-correction procedures like LASIK close to your medical unless you've confirmed the specific service's policy on post-surgical eligibility periods, which vary and change over time.

## Bottom Line

Vision and ENT are the two areas where **early, unhurried preparation** makes the biggest difference — most rejections here stem from candidates discovering a fixable issue only during the medical itself, with no time left to correct it.
