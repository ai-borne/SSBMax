# Special, Appeal and Review Medical Boards Explained (SMB, AMB, RMB)

If you're marked unfit at your first medical examination, that is **not necessarily the final word**. The system has a structured, multi-stage appeal process — understanding it removes a lot of unnecessary panic if you receive a rejection.

## Stage 1: Special Medical Board (SMB)

This is the **initial** medical board you appear before at the military hospital after being recommended at your SSB conference. It is conducted by a panel of specialists across the departments covered in the general medical overview (eyes, ENT, dental, cardiovascular, orthopaedic, etc.).

Possible outcomes:
- **Fit** — you proceed toward final selection.
- **Temporary Rejection (TR)** — a correctable issue is identified (e.g., low haemoglobin, a treatable infection, borderline weight).
- **Permanent Rejection (PR)** — a condition considered disqualifying under current medical standards.

## Stage 2: Appeal Medical Board (AMB)

If you receive a **Temporary Rejection**, you are entitled to appeal:

- You typically have **42 days** from the date of the SMB's examination to rectify the flagged issue and report for re-examination.
- **No fee** is charged for an Appeal Medical Board arising from a temporary rejection.
- The AMB is usually completed within a **single day** and focuses specifically on re-checking the condition that caused the original TR.
- Come prepared with any treatment records, follow-up test results, or specialist clearance letters relevant to the flagged condition.

If the AMB clears you, you proceed to final selection. If the AMB also finds you unfit, the next option is an RMB.

## Stage 3: Review Medical Board (RMB)

The RMB is a further level of review, generally sought after an unfavourable AMB outcome (or in specific permanent-rejection cases):

- A request for RMB is typically made **within one day** of the AMB result being communicated.
- Unlike the AMB, an RMB is **not granted automatically** — the decision to grant one rests with the **Director General, Armed Forces Medical Services (DG AFMS)**, based on the specifics of the case.
- If granted, the RMB is usually conducted at a different, senior military hospital, by a fresh panel independent of the earlier boards.

## Temporary vs. Permanent Rejection — Why the Distinction Matters

- A **Temporary Rejection (TR)** is for conditions considered correctable within a reasonable timeframe — this is the category the 42-day appeal window exists for.
- A **Permanent Rejection (PR)** relates to conditions considered disqualifying under the standing medical standards, and the appeal path (RMB) is narrower and discretionary rather than a routine next step.
- Importantly, a rejection at one SSB attempt — including a medical rejection — generally does **not** bar you from reapplying to SSB again in a future attempt through the same or another eligible entry scheme, subject to that scheme's own age and attempt-limit rules. A single medical setback is not necessarily a permanent barrier to a service career.

## Worked Example: A Temporary Rejection for Low Haemoglobin

A candidate is flagged at the SMB with haemoglobin below the required threshold — a common and highly correctable TR reason. A realistic path through the appeal process looks like this:

1. **Day 0 (SMB result)**: the candidate gets the exact haemoglobin value and threshold in writing, and confirms the 42-day window with the medical staff before leaving.
2. **Week 1**: consults a physician, who identifies mild dietary iron deficiency as the likely cause and prescribes iron supplementation alongside dietary changes (iron-rich foods, avoiding tea/coffee immediately around meals, which inhibits iron absorption).
3. **Weeks 2–5**: follows the treatment plan consistently, and gets a follow-up blood test around the 4-week mark to confirm haemoglobin is trending back into range — rather than waiting until day 41 to find out.
4. **Day 35–40**: reports for the AMB with the original SMB report, the specialist's prescription, and the follow-up test results showing correction.
5. **AMB outcome**: cleared, since the flagged value now meets the required threshold with documented evidence of the correction.

This same structure — get the exact finding in writing, treat it properly and early, document the correction, appear well within the window — applies to most correctable TR categories, not just haemoglobin.

## Common Questions About the Appeal Process

**Does an AMB or RMB cost anything?**
No fee is charged for an Appeal Medical Board arising from a temporary rejection at the SMB stage.

**Can I choose which hospital conducts my AMB?**
Generally no — the AMB is scheduled by the concerned medical authority, typically at the same or a designated military hospital, not at a location of the candidate's choosing.

**What happens if I miss the 42-day window?**
Missing the window without a valid, documented reason typically means the temporary rejection stands as-is for that attempt; candidates in this situation would need to pursue treatment and reapply for SSB in a future attempt rather than continuing the same appeal.

**Is an RMB worth requesting if I'm unsure of my chances?**
Since an RMB is discretionary and not automatically granted, it's worth requesting whenever there is a genuine basis to believe the original finding was incomplete or has since changed — but it should not be treated as a routine formality, since it depends on a case-specific review by DG AFMS.

## Practical Advice If You Receive a Temporary Rejection

1. **Get the exact reason in writing** before you leave the medical hospital — you cannot fix what you don't precisely understand.
2. **Consult a specialist immediately**, not just a general physician, for the specific flagged condition.
3. **Use the full 42-day window** productively — don't wait until the last week to start treatment.
4. **Document everything** — every test, every consultation, every prescription — the AMB panel will want to see a clear treatment trail, not just a claim that the issue is resolved.
5. **Stay realistic** about conditions that genuinely need more than 42 days to resolve (e.g., significant weight change); in such cases, focus your effort on a future, better-prepared attempt rather than forcing an AMB you're not ready for.

## Key Takeaway

The SMB → AMB → RMB structure exists precisely because medical standards recognise that some conditions are correctable and some initial findings deserve a second look. Knowing this process in advance turns a stressful, uncertain moment into a clear checklist you can act on immediately.
