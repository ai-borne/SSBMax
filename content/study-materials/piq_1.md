# How to Fill the PIQ Form: A Section-by-Section Guide

The Personal Information Questionnaire (PIQ) is filled at the very start of your SSB, usually on Day 1 before Psychology testing begins. It is one of the first documents your Interviewing Officer and Psychologist see, and it quietly shapes the questions you'll face for the rest of the process.

## Section 1: Basic Personal Details

Name, date of birth, gender, permanent and correspondence address, state, religion, mother tongue, and native place.

- Fill this **exactly** as it appears in your official documents (Aadhaar, admit card, matriculation certificate) — mismatches can cause administrative friction.
- "Native place" and "state" questions are sometimes used conversationally by the IO to open the interview — be ready to speak briefly about your hometown or region.

## Section 2: Family Background

Father's and mother's names, occupations, and educational qualifications; siblings' details (name, age, occupation/education); family income; and sometimes details of any family member who has served in the Armed Forces.

- Answer factually and completely — this section is a common source of interview questions ("Tell me about your father's work," "Does anyone in your family serve?").
- If you have a service background in the family, be ready to discuss whether that influenced your own decision to apply — this is one of the most frequently asked follow-ups.

## Section 3: Educational Record

Academic history from Class 10 onward: board/university, subjects, marks/percentage or CGPA for each level, and any academic achievements or distinctions.

- Double-check marks and percentages against your actual certificates — the IO may ask you to explain any noticeable dip or jump in performance across years.
- If your academic record has a gap year, a change of stream, or a repeated year, be prepared to explain it honestly and briefly.

## Section 4: Games, Sports, and Extra-Curricular Activities

This section is typically broken into four parts:
1. **Participation in games and sports** — school/college/state/national level, and your role (player, captain, etc.)
2. **Hobbies and interests** — what you genuinely do in your free time
3. **Extra-curricular activities** — clubs, societies, cultural or academic events you've participated in
4. **Positions of responsibility** — NCC, scouting, sports captaincy, student council, or any leadership role you've held

- List things you can **speak about in detail**, not the most impressive-sounding entries. A hobby you can discuss genuinely for five minutes is worth far more than an exaggerated interest you can't sustain a conversation about.
- Positions of responsibility are especially important — the board reads these as direct evidence of leadership and initiative, which are core Officer Like Qualities.

## Section 5: Achievements and Awards

Any academic, sporting, or extra-curricular awards or recognitions you've received.

- Be specific — name the award, the level (school/district/state/national), and the year.
- Only list what you can substantiate if asked; don't inflate a participation certificate into a "win."

## Section 6: Previous SSB Attempts (if applicable)

If this isn't your first attempt, you'll be asked to state which board, when, and at what stage you were screened out or not recommended.

- Answer honestly — this information is cross-checked, and the board is far more interested in **what you learned and changed** since your last attempt than in the fact that you didn't clear it before.

## Section 7: Career Goals and Preferences

Your preferred service (Army/Navy/Air Force), preferred arm or branch, and sometimes a brief statement of career goals or why you're applying.

- Be specific rather than generic — "I want to join the Army" is weaker than naming why a particular arm or branch appeals to you and what you understand about the role.
- Consistency matters here more than anywhere else on the form: this is the section the interview's "why do you want to join" line of questioning is built directly on top of, and it resurfaces again at the conference.

## Section 8: Self-Assessment Questions

Some PIQ versions include a short section asking you to describe your own strengths, weaknesses, or how you'd react in a specific hypothetical situation.

- Treat this the same way as the Self Description psychology exercise — genuine, specific self-reflection rather than a list of generic positive traits.
- A named weakness with a concrete example of how you're working on it is far more credible than claiming to have none.

## A Practical Filling Strategy

1. **Read the entire form once before writing anything** — understanding the full structure helps you avoid duplicating information or leaving a later section feeling repetitive.
2. **Draft mentally (or on scratch paper, if permitted) before committing ink** — especially for open-ended sections like hobbies and self-assessment, a few seconds of genuine reflection produces much better entries than the first thing that comes to mind under time pressure.
3. **Keep your handwriting clear** — assessors read many PIQs in sequence, and an illegible form makes a poor first impression before a single question is asked.
4. **Don't rush the family and educational sections** just because they feel like formalities — as covered above, they're frequently used to open interview conversation.

## Why the PIQ Matters More Than Candidates Expect

Many candidates treat the PIQ as paperwork to get through quickly before the "real" evaluation begins. In practice, it is read closely by both the IO and the Psychologist before they ever meet you, and it directly shapes the specific questions you'll be asked — which means the effort you put into it has a compounding effect on the quality of your entire SSB experience, not just a single form.

## General Filling Tips

- Write legibly and in the language/medium instructed; avoid overwriting or excessive corrections.
- Keep entries **factual and verifiable** — nearly every line on this form can become an interview question.
- Fill it calmly and honestly rather than rushing — this is the foundation document the rest of your SSB conversation is built on.
