# Conference Etiquette and Making the Right Final Impression

The conference is brief, but it is still an observed interaction — the board is watching how you carry yourself one last time, even though the substantive evaluation is already largely complete.

## Dress and Grooming

- Wear the same formal attire expected throughout SSB: pressed shirt and trousers, polished shoes, and a tie if that is the convention at your centre.
- Grooming should be neat and understated — a clean shave or trimmed beard, tidy hair, no strong fragrances.
- Avoid anything new or untested on this day; conference morning is not the time to try a new outfit or shoes you haven't broken in.

## Entering and Exiting the Room

- Walk in with a steady, unhurried gait — the walk itself is part of the impression, just as it was in your interview and GTO tasks.
- Greet the board with a simple, respectful acknowledgement (a nod or a brief "Good morning, Sir") — do not launch into unsolicited speech before being addressed.
- Stand at ease unless invited to sit; posture should be relaxed but upright, not stiff or slouched.
- When dismissed, thank the board briefly and leave calmly — don't linger or try to extend the interaction.

## Eye Contact and Body Language

- Make eye contact with whoever is speaking to you, and briefly acknowledge other board members rather than fixating on just the President.
- Keep your hands still and relaxed; avoid fidgeting, adjusting your clothes repeatedly, or crossing your arms.
- A calm, composed expression communicates more than words in a two-minute interaction — the board has spent four days assessing your emotional stability, and this is a final data point, not the deciding one.

## Honesty Over Performance

- **Do not try to "sell" yourself in the conference.** By this stage, any obvious attempt to project qualities you haven't demonstrated over the previous four days reads as inconsistent, which is far more damaging than a modest, honest answer.
- If asked a question you don't have a strong answer to, it is better to give a short, genuine response than to improvise something that sounds impressive but rings false.
- Gratitude is welcome but should be brief and sincere — a single sentence thanking the board for the opportunity is sufficient; effusive or repeated thanks can feel performative.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Over-explaining** when asked about an inconsistency — a short, honest clarification is stronger than a long justification.
- **Rehearsed answers** that don't match your natural speaking style from the rest of the week — assessors notice the shift in tone.
- **Reading the room incorrectly** — a long pause from the board before dismissing you is not necessarily a bad sign; deliberation cues during the brief interaction are not reliable indicators of outcome.
- **Discussing other candidates** unprompted, or comparing yourself to them — keep the focus entirely on your own answers.

## Managing Nerves in the Waiting Period

The wait before your turn is often more stressful than the conference itself — you have nothing to do but sit with your batchmates and anticipate. A few practical approaches:

- Use slow, controlled breathing rather than trying to distract yourself completely; you want to arrive alert, not artificially relaxed.
- Avoid last-minute "strategy" conversations with other candidates about what to say — at this stage, second-guessing your own genuine answers based on someone else's opinion tends to make responses sound less natural, not more polished.
- Remind yourself of the simple, factual point that the conference is a review, not a new test — there is genuinely nothing new to prepare for in these final minutes.

## Handling an Unexpected or Difficult Question

Occasionally a board member asks something you haven't anticipated — about a specific incident, a family matter, or a direct challenge to something you said earlier in the week. The etiquette that matters most here:

- Take a brief, visible pause rather than rushing to fill silence; a short "Let me think about that for a moment, Sir" is entirely acceptable.
- Answer the actual question asked, as directly as you can, rather than pivoting to a more comfortable topic.
- If you genuinely don't know or can't answer well, say so plainly rather than improvising — a calm "I'm not certain about that, Sir" preserves credibility far better than a fabricated answer that doesn't hold up.

## After You Leave the Room

Once dismissed, your conduct is effectively no longer observed by the board, but maintaining the same composure while rejoining your batch matters for a subtler reason: candidates frequently discuss their conference experience with each other while waiting for results, and projecting either excessive confidence or visible distress can affect your own composure heading into the result declaration, which is itself still part of the observed setting at many centres.

## The Bigger Picture

Every officer who has been through SSB will tell you the same thing: the conference feels anticlimactic precisely because it *is* — the real evaluation happened across Psychology, GTO, and Interview. Walking in calm, being honest, and treating it as a natural continuation of how you've behaved all week is the only "strategy" that consistently works.
