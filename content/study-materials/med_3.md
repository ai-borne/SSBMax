# Common Medical Disqualifications and How to Avoid Them

Most medical rejections at SSB fall into a small number of recurring categories. Knowing them in advance lets you fix what's fixable long before you're standing in front of a medical board.

## 1. Body Mass and Height-Weight Mismatch

- The Armed Forces use a standard **height-weight chart**; being significantly over or under the proportionate range for your height is flagged.
- **Fix**: start a structured fitness and nutrition routine at least 3–6 months before SSB, not in the final weeks — sudden crash dieting or bulking can itself cause other flagged issues (low haemoglobin, abnormal vitals).

## 2. Vision and Refractive Errors

- Covered in depth in the companion material on vision/ENT standards — myopia beyond the permitted limit is one of the single most common rejection reasons, especially for flying branches.
- **Fix**: get an eye exam early; know your exact numbers before you apply, so you choose an entry/branch you're actually eligible for.

## 3. Dental Issues

- Multiple untreated cavities, significant misalignment, or missing teeth beyond the permitted count can be flagged.
- **Fix**: a dental check-up and any necessary treatment 2–3 months in advance is usually enough — this is one of the most easily preventable rejection categories.

## 4. Skin Conditions

- Active, extensive, or chronic skin conditions (certain forms of eczema, psoriasis, vitiligo beyond permitted extent) can be disqualifying depending on severity and location.
- Tattoos are subject to **service-specific policies** — location, size, and content all matter, and policies differ across Army, Navy, and Air Force and are updated periodically, so verify current rules for your specific entry before getting new ink.
- **Fix**: treat active conditions well ahead of time; if you already have tattoos, check the current policy for your target service before applying rather than assuming.

## 5. Orthopaedic Issues

- Old fractures that healed with deformity, significant scoliosis, flat feet (pes planus) beyond a certain grade, or restricted joint movement are commonly flagged.
- **Fix**: if you have a history of a sports injury or old fracture, get it evaluated by an orthopaedist beforehand so you know whether it's likely to be an issue, and carry your treatment records to the medical.

## 6. Low Haemoglobin / Abnormal Blood Values

- Often a **temporary** rejection reason, especially in candidates who have been dieting aggressively or are otherwise mildly anaemic.
- **Fix**: eat a balanced, iron-adequate diet in the weeks leading up to SSB; avoid extreme calorie restriction right before your medical.

## 7. Cardiac and Respiratory Findings

- An abnormal ECG or chest X-ray finding, even if ultimately benign, will typically require further specialist evaluation before clearance.
- **Fix**: if you have any known cardiac or respiratory history (asthma, a childhood murmur, etc.), get a current specialist opinion in advance rather than being surprised at the medical.

## 8. Undisclosed Medical History

- Board doctors specifically look for **inconsistencies** between your declared medical history and what they find — an undisclosed old surgery or condition discovered during examination is treated far more seriously than the same condition disclosed upfront.
- **Fix**: always declare your medical history accurately and completely, even if you're unsure whether something is relevant. Honesty here is assessed independently of the medical condition itself.

## General Preparation Timeline

| Timeframe before SSB | Action |
|---|---|
| 3–6 months | Eye exam, dental check-up, address any known chronic condition |
| 1–3 months | Build/maintain fitness routine, balanced diet, resolve minor infections |
| 2 weeks | Adequate sleep, avoid crash diets or sudden intense new exercise |
| Day of medical | Carry all existing prescriptions, reports, and treatment records |

The common thread across nearly every avoidable rejection is **time** — issues discovered and treated months in advance are rarely a problem; the same issues discovered for the first time at the medical board often are.
